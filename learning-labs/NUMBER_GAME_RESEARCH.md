# Number game: research and learning design

The number game is Learning Labs' early maths game for children aged 5–8 (K–2): the foundations of number, practised a few minutes at a time, at home or in a small group. It follows the shared principles in `PRINCIPLES.md`. This document covers what it teaches, in what order, and why.

Draft, 2026-11-20 (first pass). Numbers come from published abstracts and study pages; items marked *(verify)* need checking against the full paper before citing.

## Why early number matters

- **Early maths is the strongest predictor of later achievement.** Across six large longitudinal datasets, maths skills at school entry predicted later achievement (ages 7–14) more strongly than early reading or attention skills, and predicted later reading about as well as early reading did (Duncan et al., 2007).
- **The gap starts before school.** Children from low-income families arrive with less numerical knowledge, partly from fewer chances to learn maths at home or in early settings (NRC, 2009; Siegler & Ramani, 2008).
- **So the problem it solves:** building the foundations of number (counting, how many, comparing, and putting numbers together and apart), well practised and on time, so later maths has something to stand on.

## What to teach: the number core

The National Research Council's review named **number** (whole numbers, operations and relations) as one of the two core areas of early maths, with geometry, spatial relations and measurement as the other (NRC, 2009). The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide for ages 3–6 makes five recommendations (Frye et al., 2013):

1. **Teach number and operations using a developmental progression.** The only one rated "moderate" evidence; the rest are "minimal".
2. Teach geometry, patterns, measurement and data analysis, also in a progression.
3. Use progress monitoring so teaching builds on what each child knows.
4. Teach children to see and describe their world mathematically.
5. Dedicate time to maths each day.

**For the number game:** focus on number and operations (recommendation 1, the best supported), follow a developmental progression, and use the mastery data the way recommendation 3 describes. Geometry and patterns could follow later as their own strand.

## In what order: learning trajectories

Clements and Sarama describe early maths as **learning trajectories**: a goal, a developmental progression of levels children pass through, and activities matched to each level. For number, the progression runs roughly:

**recognising small amounts at a glance** (subitizing) → **saying the counting words in order** → **counting objects, one number for each, and knowing the last number tells how many** (cardinality) → **comparing** (more, fewer, the same) → **putting together and taking apart small numbers** (composing and decomposing: 5 is 2 and 3) → **adding and subtracting** with objects, then with strategies → **tens and ones** (place value).

Their Building Blocks curriculum, built on these trajectories, produced larger gains than comparison classrooms across counting, subitizing, sequencing and addition (Clements & Sarama, 2007; a large cluster randomised trial, Clements et al., 2011 *(verify effect sizes)*).

A short **number sense intervention** for kindergartners from low-income communities (small groups, 8 weeks, covering counting, number recognition, comparing and simple adding and subtracting) outperformed controls on number skills and general maths, with gains that lasted (Jordan et al., 2012, randomised controlled trial *(verify sample size)*). This is close to what the number game's first band would practise.

## A path, not a line

- **Straight-line number board games build number sense.** Preschoolers from low-income programs who played a simple board game with spaces numbered 1–10 in a row, for four 15-minute sessions over two weeks, improved their numerical knowledge; children who played the same game with colours instead of numbers didn't (Siegler & Ramani, 2008; small, n = 36, but it meets What Works Clearinghouse standards). A related study found gains in comparing numbers, placing them on a line, counting and naming numerals, still there nine weeks later (Ramani & Siegler, 2008).
- **Number paths before number lines.** A number path is a row of numbered spaces, each one counted, like a board game. A number line measures distances between tick marks, which young children find hard. Early maths practitioners recommend number paths for preschool and kindergarten (Erikson Institute; NAEYC). *(Practitioner guidance; we found no head-to-head study.)*

**For the number game:** a number path is a natural spine. The map can be one, and so can game screens (move along the path, find where a number lives, count on).

## Digital practice: what works

- **Apps help modestly, most on basic number knowledge.** Across 36 rigorous studies of apps for preschool to grade 3, the average gain was +0.31 SD, similar in maths and literacy, larger for "constrained" skills such as basic number knowledge and on researcher-designed measures than on standardised tests (Kim et al., 2021). Basic number skills are also what most maths apps target, and published app quality ratings didn't predict learning (Outhwaite et al., 2023 *(verify)*).
- **Virtual manipulatives** (objects children move or tap on screen) had a moderate effect compared with other teaching (about 0.34 SD in one count *(verify: the two reports of the 2013 meta-analysis give different study counts)*). Two features that helped are directly useful here: **simultaneous linking** (showing objects, numerals and words together, changing together) and **focused constraint** (letting the child do only what the lesson is about) (Moyer-Packenham & Westenskow, 2013, 2016).

**For the number game:** show objects, the numeral and the spoken number **at the same moment** (this is also the cognitive load principle "show and say together"); keep interactions simple taps; build tests of learning that match what's practised.

## Motivation and feelings about maths

- **Maths anxiety starts early.** In 154 first and second graders, maths anxiety went with lower achievement for children with stronger working memory (Ramirez et al., 2013). There's no direct test of timed drills causing it in young children, but there's no need for timers to learn these skills either. **No timers, no speed scores.**
- **Praise the process.** Kindergartners given person praise ("you're good at this") showed more helpless responses after a later mistake than those praised for their process (Kamins & Dweck, 1999).

## Grade bands

One number game with three bands inside, like Word Path. Number skills build continuously, so one sequence keeps placement, progress and the report together; each band can still feel like its own world. Band content follows the learning trajectories above and the Common Core maths standards (free to use; check the official text before citing exact wording).

| Band | Focus | Examples of what children do |
|---|---|---|
| **K** | Counting and how many to 10, then 20; recognising numerals; subitizing to 5; comparing; making 5 and 10 (composing and decomposing); adding and subtracting within 10, quick within 5 | Count the apples and pop the number; which has more?; how many more make 5?; find 7 on the path |
| **Grade 1** | Adding and subtracting within 20 with strategies (make ten, doubles); tens and ones; counting to 120; comparing two-digit numbers | Make a ten to add 8 + 5; how many tens and ones?; jump along the path in tens |
| **Grade 2** | Adding and subtracting within 100; place value to 1000; quick recall within 20; skip counting | Add 37 + 25 with tens and ones; skip count by 5s |

**The prototype builds the K band only.**

## Possible game types (K band), mapped to Word Path's mechanics

| Skill | Game idea | Word Path mechanic it reuses |
|---|---|---|
| Subitizing | **Quick look:** a dot pattern or ten frame shows; the child taps the number they see | Pop (tap the right bubble) |
| Counting, cardinality | **Count it:** objects light up one by one as each number is said; then "how many?" | Show and say together (like Blend it's letters) |
| Numerals | **Find the number:** hear a number, pop its numeral | Word pop |
| Comparing | **More or fewer:** two groups; which has more? | Sort (two bins) |
| Number path | **Where does it live?** tap where 6 goes on the path | Pick a picture |
| Composing to 5 and 10 | **Make 5:** a five frame with 3 dots; how many more? | Pop |

**Every game type keeps to the shared principles:** spoken instructions, show and say together, hints that grow, practise what was missed, first tries count, no timers.

## Open questions

- **Subitizing without a timer:** subitizing means seeing an amount without counting, which usually needs a brief look. Can a short "peek" (the child chooses when to look again) work without feeling like a time limit?
- **Pictures:** what objects to count (consistent, culturally neutral, not distracting), and whether to use dots and ten frames more than pictures as children advance.
- **Recordings:** number words 1–20 and a small set of lines. Much smaller than Word Path's.
- **Evaluation:** a short before-and-after check of counting, numeral recognition and comparing, as Word Path plans (R8).
