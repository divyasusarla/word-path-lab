# Learning Labs: shared principles

Learning Labs is a family of small, calm learning games for children aged 5–8: **Word Path** (reading: sounds into words) and a **number game** (early maths; name to be decided). Each game has its own content and research (Word Path: `LEARNING_DESIGN.md`; number game: `NUMBER_GAME_RESEARCH.md`). This page holds what they share: how they teach, how they talk to children, and what they never do.

Draft, 2026-11-20. Every principle below traces to a source in the references or in the two research documents.

## Who it's for

- Children aged 5–8 (kindergarten to grade 2), in **grade bands**: each game is one continuous sequence, and a grown-up chooses where a child starts.
- Used **individually at home** or in **small groups in class** with a teacher. Group use is designed for from the start (teacher picks the level, the group answers together), even where it isn't built yet.
- The grown-up is part of the design: they set up the sound, choose the band, read the progress report, and are the only one who can reach settings.

## How the games teach

| # | Principle | In practice | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Follow a developmental sequence** | Content is ordered as children typically learn it (Word Path: a phonics teaching order; number game: learning trajectories). New material builds on what was just learned | NRP (2000); Frye et al. (2013), rec. 1; Clements & Sarama (2014) |
| 2 | **Respect working memory** | One new thing at a time. Short rounds, short levels, sessions of about 10–15 minutes | Baddeley & Hitch (1974); Mayer & Moreno (2003): segmenting |
| 3 | **Show and say together** | Spoken words play at the same moment as what they describe (letters light up as each sound plays; objects light up as they're counted) | Mayer & Moreno (2003): temporal contiguity, modality |
| 4 | **Nothing that isn't the lesson** | No background music, mascots, characters or decorative animation, above all at the moment a child is deciding | Mayer & Moreno (2003): coherence; Kirschner et al. on seductive details |
| 5 | **Point at what matters** | Highlight the part being learned: the tricky letters in a heart word, the "ten" in a ten frame | Mayer & Moreno (2003): signaling |
| 6 | **Easy first, support fades** | Few, clearly different choices at first; closer, harder contrasts once a child shows they're ready. Help is automatic early on and offered later | Martin (2016); Martin et al. (2025): load reduction instruction; Kalyuga (2022): expertise reversal |
| 7 | **Practise what was missed, spread over days** | Missed items come back in the same play; mastery needs first-try success on more than one day; earlier material is reviewed | Cepeda et al. (2006); Word Path R4 |
| 8 | **Judge progress by first tries** | Only a child's first answer counts towards mastery; a hint or a shown answer never makes something look known | Frye et al. (2013), rec. 3 (progress monitoring) |
| 9 | **A little help first, more if needed** | After a miss: one wrong choice is taken away and a game-specific hint plays. After a second: the answer is shown and said | Hattie & Timperley (2007); graded prompting in early intervention |
| 10 | **Tap, don't drag** | Simple taps on large targets. Dragging is optional at most | Skulmowski & Xu (2022) on interaction cost |
| 11 | **Audio first** | Every instruction is spoken in a real voice; children who can't read yet can play alone | Word Path testing; Mayer & Moreno (2003): modality |

## How the games talk to children

- **Say what was right**, not just "Great!": "That says sh." "You counted seven." Informational feedback supports motivation (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999).
- **Praise the doing, never the child.** "You kept going" or "You looked carefully", never "You're so smart". Praise aimed at the person leaves young children more helpless after a setback than praise aimed at the process (Kamins & Dweck, 1999; caveat: two studies from one lab).
- **Mistakes are normal.** "Let's look again." Never "Wrong!", buzzers or red crosses.
- **Calm, warm and short.** No hype, no exclamation-heavy celebration, no pressure.
- **One line at a time.** A new line stops the one before; nothing talks over the child.

## What Learning Labs never does

- **No timers, countdowns or speed scores** in early maths or reading. Maths anxiety appears as early as grades 1–2, and in 154 first and second graders it went with lower achievement for children with stronger working memory (Ramirez et al., 2013). The link to timed tests hasn't been tested directly, so we leave them out as a precaution.
- **No leaderboards, streaks that can be lost, lives, or anything that punishes stopping.**
- **No ads, purchases or links** a child can reach. Anything that leaves the game sits behind the grown-up gate.
- **No tracking.** Nothing leaves the device, no accounts, no outside services. Progress is saved on the device only.
- **No overriding the family's settings:** for example, sound never plays through a device's silent mode; the game asks instead.
- **No characters, avatars or background music** (principle 4).

## Rewards

Stickers stay light: one per level, collected, never the point. Expected, tangible rewards for completing tasks can reduce children's intrinsic motivation; informational feedback increases it (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999). Research item R9 revisits this with testing.

## What the evidence promises, and what it doesn't

Apps for preschool to grade 3 show **modest** average gains (+0.31 SD), largest for "constrained" skills like letter sounds and basic number knowledge, and smaller on standardised tests (Kim et al., 2021). A game supplements teaching; it doesn't replace it. Engagement isn't evidence of learning, so each game needs its own simple before-and-after check before claiming impact (Word Path R8).

## Shared parts (for building)

Written once, used by both games: sound and recordings; saved progress and mastery; hints; the grown-up gate, sound check and progress report; the visual design (type, colour, buttons); the "show and say together" patterns. Each game keeps its own content and game types.
